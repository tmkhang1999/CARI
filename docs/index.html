TL;DR

Train on two lights, predict from one

  • The problem. One photograph cannot tell a dark surface from a dimly lit one, so part of the light leaks into the recovered albedo.
  • The idea. Two photographs of one scene under different light go through the same network, and their albedos must agree. The pairs are used only in training, so inference is still one image and one pass.
  • The result. In a matched ablation, CIAI lowers the luminance variation of a material across lighting by 28–39%, and the albedo error on raw ARAP renderings by 7–14%, with 18.5M trainable parameters.
  • The limit. Colour improves much less, because the explanation loss uses luminance only. The next stage gives the shading colour its own output.
Contributions
  1. A paired training strategy on real MID photographs with pseudo-ground-truth albedo, added as a second training stage.
  2. A matched ablation that isolates the effect of CIAI on luminance stability and on albedo accuracy.
  3. An evaluation that pairs stability with accuracy, because a grey, constant albedo is perfectly stable.
Method

Cross-Illumination Albedo Invariance

Write a photograph as I = A ⊙ Sd + R: albedo times shading, plus a residual for highlights. From one image, a grey wall in shadow and a dark wall in full light are the same pixels, so whatever the model cannot attribute to the light ends up in the albedo. Here, "material colour" means the diffuse linear-RGB albedo, not the spectral reflectance.

Figure 1. Existing models under two lights. CRefNet stays similar across the two lights partly because its albedo is pale, and Marigold-App keeps more colour but shifts with the light.

A second photograph of the same scene under different light breaks the tie: the surfaces are the same in both, so anything that changed is lighting. This follows Siamese learning and multi-view consistency in 3D reconstruction, with positive pairs only. We use the Multi-Illumination Dataset (MID), which photographs every scene 25 times with a flash bounced in a different direction, and the pseudo-ground-truth albedo that MIDIntrinsics derives for all 25 frames.

Figure 2. One MID pair. Same camera, same objects, the flash bounced in a different direction. The flash is white, so the light mainly changes in direction and intensity.

Figure 3. CIAI at training time. One shared model, two passes. The thumbnails are outputs of our model.

Linv albedo invariance
the pair must agree
Masked L1 between the two RGB albedos over pixels that are valid in both frames (not clipped, not near black).
Lexpl shading explanation
the shading must explain the change
The log luminance ratio of the two shadings must equal that of the two images. It rules out a flat albedo, and it sees brightness only.

The model is a frozen DINOv2-L encoder with a DPT decoder, an albedo head and a three-channel shading head (18.5M trainable parameters, 0.148 s per 512×512 image). It is first trained with supervised losses on Hypersim and InteriorVerse, and CIAI is added as a second stage on raw MID pairs. Linv says "agree across lights", and the supervised losses say what that agreement should look like; agreement alone could also be satisfied by the same wrong albedo in both frames.

The frozen encoder tends to wash out the albedo colour, so the model also has a colour path: an RGB skip into the albedo head and a chroma loss on albedo. This is a design choice of the model, not part of CIAI, and the skip can pass the light colour of the input into the albedo as a colour cast.

  1. CIAI improves luminance stability. Cmat drops by 28% and 39%, while hue drift (Castrel) improves only by 6% and 4%.
  2. CIAI improves accuracy where the input carries its lighting. On raw ARAP renderings, albedo LMSE drops by 14% and 7%; after white balance the change is small. ARAP is never used in training.
  3. CIAI does not improve MAW. ΔE goes from 4.43 to 4.63 and from 4.12 to 4.16.
Limitations

What CIAI does not fix

  • Colour. The explanation loss uses luminance only, and MID's flash is white, so the light colour changes only slightly between frames (median chromaticity gap 0.030). As a result, our hue drift still grows with the light colour, as in a grey-shading model.
  • Reflective surfaces. An albedo–shading product cannot represent a mirror, so the reflection moves into the albedo as the light moves (Figure 6).
  • Hard shadows. A sharp cast shadow can still be read as a material boundary.
  • Provisional numbers. The ARAP numbers use an evaluation mask that fails on ARAP's mixed ground-truth encodings, and the images on this page come from a later checkpoint of the same model. Both will be regenerated.

Figure 6. Reflective surfaces. The mirror sphere from the teaser: its reflections change with the light in the input and in our albedo.

Next steps

From luminance to colour

The next model splits the shading into a grey part and a colour part, so that a change in light colour has a place to go other than the albedo. It is planned and not trained yet.

Figure 7. Planned pipeline. The output panels are ground-truth targets from a rendered 3D-Front scene (S = I / A), not predictions. Light-grey pixels are masked, and chroma is shown at constant luminance.

  1. Cross-architecture test. Train with and without CIAI on a CNN, a fine-tuned ViT and a single-step diffusion model, with the same data and three seeds.
  2. Model. Predict I = A ⊙ (Slum · C) + R, and feed the albedo head the corrected image I / (Slum · C) instead of raw RGB.
  3. Loss. Add a chroma explanation loss on C, used only on pairs whose light colour really changes.
  4. Data. Rendered coloured-light pairs from 3D-FRONT with exact albedo, and real coloured-light pairs such as LSMI.
