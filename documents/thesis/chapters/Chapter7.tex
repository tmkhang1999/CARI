\chapter{Conclusion}
\label{ch:conclusion}

\section{Summary}

This report studied Cross-Illumination Albedo Invariance (CIAI), a training strategy for
intrinsic image decomposition in which two photographs of the same scene under different
illumination must yield the same albedo, and the shading must explain the change in
brightness between them. We added it as a second training stage to a feed-forward model
with a frozen DINOv2-Large encoder, a DPT decoder and three-channel shading, using real
raw pairs from the Multi-Illumination Dataset and the MIDIntrinsics pseudo-ground truth.

The strategy does what its training signal supports. MID's pairs differ mainly in the
direction and intensity of a white flash, and the explanation loss is defined on
luminance; correspondingly, CIAI reduces the variation of recovered material lightness
across illuminations by $28\%$ and $39\%$ in matched ablations, and it lowers the dense
albedo error on raw, coloured-lit ARAP renderings by $7$--$14\%$, a benchmark outside its
training data. Against other methods our model is more lightness-stable than CD-IID, both
Marigold variants and Ordinal Shading, level with CRefNet and behind RGB$\rightarrow$X, while
training $18.5$M parameters. It is behind CD-IID on in-domain chroma error and mid-table on
MAW.

The strategy does not deliver colour stability. Hue drift improves only slightly under
CIAI, and our model's drift grows with the illuminant colour as much as a grey-shading
method's, which is what a luminance-only explanation loss and MID's white flash predict. The
method that does show colour-independent drift, CD-IID, predicts the shading colour in a
dedicated stage, which suggests a structural remedy: a model that separates shading
luminance from shading chroma, planned below.

The colour path of the base model is a design choice that restores the albedo colour the
frozen encoder tends to wash out, and it is separate from CIAI. Every stability result here
is reported beside an accuracy result, because a grey, constant albedo is perfectly stable.


\section{Limitations}

\paragraph{Colour}
The main limitation is that the trained model does not separate illuminant colour from
material colour reliably. The explanation loss uses luminance only, and the real training
pairs vary colour over a median gap of $0.030$; the stability benchmark measures hue drift
over the same narrow range, so the colour results are both a modelling result and a
measurement limit.

\paragraph{One architecture, single runs}
CIAI is presented as a training strategy, but it has been tested on one architecture, a
frozen-encoder regression model. Whether its lightness gain holds for convolutional,
fine-tuned transformer or diffusion models is untested. Most comparisons are single
training runs, so small differences should not be read as effects.

\paragraph{Benchmarks}
The ARAP results were computed with a mask that fails on ARAP's mixed ground-truth
encodings and are provisional (\secref{sec:arap_results}). MID is in the training data of
both our model and CD-IID, so no MID comparison is zero-shot, and its pseudo-ground truth
is itself derived from the capture stack.

\paragraph{Single-image accuracy}
CIAI buys stability, not single-image accuracy: on MAW it slightly worsens both measures,
and on IIW the model trails most baselines. A stronger base model, or the cross-architecture
study below, is needed before the strategy can be judged on accuracy.

\paragraph{Achromatic ambiguity and non-diffuse surfaces}
Hard cast shadows can still be read as material, and mirrors, glass and glossy plastic are
unstable across illuminations because an albedo--shading product has no representation for
them; the residual absorbs only part of their appearance. \figref{fig:limit_reflective} shows
the mirror sphere of a MID scene: its reflections move with the flash in the input and, in the
same way, in our albedo.

\section{Future Work}

The next stage of the project follows directly from the findings above. It has five parts,
ordered so that each result is interpretable before the next is built on it.

\paragraph{1. Show that CIAI is a training strategy, not a property of one decoder}
Train each of three architectures with and without the CIAI stage, from the same
supervised initialisation, on the same data and for the same number of steps, with three
seeds per arm: a convolutional U-Net with an ImageNet-pretrained encoder; a DPT decoder on
a \emph{fine-tuned} DINOv2 encoder; and a latent-diffusion decomposer (Marigold-IID) adapted
to single-step deterministic inference \mycite{martingarcia25e2eft}, which makes the
prediction a differentiable function of the input so that a paired loss can be applied to
it. The claim to test is a consistent reduction in $C_{\text{mat}}$ without loss on MAW.

\paragraph{2. A shading model that separates luminance from chroma}
Predict the image as
\begin{equation}
  \mathbf{I} = \mathbf{A} \odot \bigl(S_{\text{lum}}\,\mathbf{C}\bigr) + \mathbf{R},
  \qquad Y(\mathbf{C}) = 1 ,
  \label{eq:trifactor}
\end{equation}
with a scalar shading luminance $S_{\text{lum}}$, a unit-luminance shading chroma map
$\mathbf{C}$ predicted by its own head with a low-frequency and a global component, and
the albedo $\mathbf{A}$. This follows the separation that makes CD-IID colour-stable, inside
a single network (\texttt{src/models/trifactor\_net.py}). The colour path changes accordingly:
the albedo head receives the illuminant-corrected image
$\mathbf{I} / (S_{\text{lum}}\mathbf{C})$ rather than the raw input, so that material colour
reaches it and illuminant colour does not. A three-row ablation (raw skip, corrected skip,
no skip, each with the chroma loss on albedo) decides whether the corrected skip is better.

\paragraph{3. Losses matched to the factors}
Keep $\mathcal{L}_{\text{inv}}$ on the albedo and apply the luminance explanation loss to
$S_{\text{lum}}$. Add a chroma explanation loss on $\mathbf{C}$. With
$\mathbf{r} = \log(\mathbf{x}_1+\varepsilon) - \log(\mathbf{x}_2+\varepsilon)$ per channel and
$c(\mathbf{r}) = \mathbf{r} - \tfrac{1}{3}\sum_c r_c$ its chromatic part, it asks
$c(\mathbf{r}_{\mathbf{C}})$ to match $c(\mathbf{r}_{\mathbf{I}})$ over the valid pixels, which
scores exactly the component that \eqnref{eq:lexpl} discards and can only be satisfied by moving
colour into the chroma factor. Apply it only to pairs whose measured illuminant gap exceeds
a threshold (for example $0.08$), since on near-neutral pairs it would fit noise. Supervise $\mathbf{C}$ directly wherever ground truth exists.

\paragraph{4. Training pairs whose illuminant colour actually varies}
A rendered corpus built from 3D-FRONT covers the range from near-neutral to strongly coloured pairs
(tenth to ninetieth percentile gap $0.008$ to $0.35$) with exact albedo and shading chroma;
its pilot fails only a hard-shadow quality gate, which should not block its use for
colour. Real coloured pairs are the other half: the LSMI dataset \mycite{kim21lsmi}
photographs each scene under several combinations of two or three illuminants and provides
per-pixel illuminant chromaticity, which yields both cross-illumination pairs and a direct
target for $\mathbf{C}$. If neither transfers, a small capture of our own with coloured
filters on an MID-style rig would provide pixel-aligned real pairs with known colour
changes.

\paragraph{5. Evaluation on a real colour range}
Regenerate ARAP with encoding-aware masks; report hue drift on the ARAP pairs whose
illuminant colour changes by at least $0.15$ (26 pairs in 17 scenes), on held-out LSMI
scenes and on held-out 3D-Front pairs, always beside MAW; and report the absolute drift
together with the relative one, so that a reduction in drift accompanied by a loss of
colour is visible in both.

\paragraph{Applications}
\chapref{ch:practice} demonstrated qualitative factor-space edits. Once the shading chroma
is a separate factor, relighting and white-balance edits become direct operations on
$\mathbf{C}$, and evaluating them quantitatively, for example by user study, becomes
worthwhile.
